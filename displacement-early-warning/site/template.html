San Francisco Bay Area · 1,550 census tracts · 1990–2026

Displacement pressure is predictable. It is not one thing.

A simple model trained on year-2000 census data picks out the neighborhoods that went on to lose low-income households far better than the published vulnerability rule. Since 2019 the pressure has split: house flipping stayed in lower-income neighborhoods while price and rent growth moved to affluent ones. A single risk score hides that, and every model here is weakest for some of the people it is meant to protect.

1 · Following the households

One in five tracts lost low-income households while the region gained them

Between 2000 and 2018 the nine counties gained a net 228,345 low-income households. Yet 292 of 1,550 tracts ended the period with fewer than they started with, and those tracts lost 24,521 between them. The loss is concentrated in San Francisco and Alameda County.

The outcome used throughout is the Urban Displacement Project (UDP) count of households under 80% of area median income. It is a proxy: it cannot see anyone move, and it also falls when incomes rise in place.

2 · Testing the early-warning idea on history

Conditions in 2000 predicted the loss. The published rule did not.

We gave three models 19 census measures from 1990 and 2000 and asked which tracts would lose low-income households by 2018. A quarter of the tracts were set aside before any modelling and never used for tuning.

All three models land between 0.78 and 0.79 and cannot be told apart on 388 test tracts. The UDP vulnerability flag for 2000 scores 0.52, barely better than a coin flip. The flag was built to describe neighborhood stages, so this shows what a fitted model adds, not a fault in the typology.

How well each approach ranks the test tracts

AUC on 388 held-out tracts, 73 of which lost households. White lines show 95% intervals.

Source: E2. Logistic regression was chosen by county-held-out cross-validation on the other 1,162 tracts and is used in the rest of the page. After recalibration the riskiest fifth of test tracts was predicted at 52% and observed at 46%.

The strongest signals in 2000 were a high share of low-income households in places that were already expensive and well educated. Losing low-income households in the 1990s told us nothing about the next two decades.

Does the result survive a stricter definition? The base label counts a tract that lost a single household. We retrained on two tougher versions with the same test tracts. The model holds up or improves, and the gap to the rule stays.

3 · Following the market after 2018

After 2018 the pressure split in two

The census cannot yet tell us who was displaced after 2018, so the forward test predicts market pressure instead: where flipping, price appreciation and rent growth were highest in 2019 to 2026. These are signals that can lead to displacement. They are not displacement.

Zillow price histories for 144,661 homes were placed into tracts to build those outcomes. Features come from 2012 and 2018 census data plus Zillow events dated before 2019, and whole map blocks were held out for testing.

Predicting the top quarter of tracts on three kinds of pressure

The UDP flag points the right way for flipping and the wrong way for the other two: tracts it marks as vulnerable were less likely than others to see the fastest price or rent growth. Across tracts the three outcomes barely move together.

The neighborhoods that rose fastest before 2019 cooled the most. Oakland's 94606 went from +23% a year to −2%. Growth since then is led by Atherton, Saratoga and Los Altos. A model that treats rising prices as the displacement signal would now point at the wrong places.

4 · Following the rent

Rents are furthest out of reach where incomes are lowest

In tracts UDP classes as ongoing displacement or at risk of gentrification, the median asking rent today equals about 60% of the tract's median household income. In exclusive tracts it is 29%. Incomes are in 2018 dollars and rents are current, so the ratios overstate the burden by an unknown amount. If every income had risen 30%, rent would still pass the 30% guideline in nine of ten low-income tracts.

Re-listings that rose more than 10% a year

Same listing advertised again one to six years later.

Source: E8, 36,320 listing pairs. For houses, condos and townhomes alone the regional share is 8.1%; apartment listings can cover a whole building.

California's AB 1482 caps increases for a sitting tenant at 5% plus inflation, to a maximum of 10%, and sets no limit on the rent of a vacant unit. A jump above 10% between listings is therefore legal. It measures what a household would pay to rent back into the same home.

5 · Following the errors

The models are weakest where the stakes are highest

With one regional threshold, the backtest model finds most losses in renter-heavy tracts near rail and misses most of them in owner-heavy suburbs. It caught none of the seven loss tracts in Solano County and none of the eight in Napa. Renter tracts also carry the false alarms.

Limits

  • The outcome counts low-income households. Rising incomes, students, new construction and tract growth all move it, and it does not observe anyone moving out.
  • The backtest covers one 18-year period. It shows what was predictable then, not that the same relationships hold now.
  • The forward test predicts market pressure. Whether that pressure displaced anyone needs newer census data.
  • Zillow histories exist only for homes listed or sold recently. Features dated before 2019 describe the past of today's market sample.
  • Rents are asking rents, not signed leases, and most rental events date from 2024 to 2026.
  • A flip is defined from price and timing alone. Renovation, new construction and family transfers cannot be separated out.
  • Several groups have fewer than ten positive cases, including San Francisco in the flipping test set. Read those rows as indications.
  • The first draw of the E5 test blocks contained no San Francisco tract and was redrawn after results had been seen. Flipping results held on both draws.
  • Race is recorded only as a non-white share, so the comparison is people of color against white.
  • Tree-based models differ in the fourth decimal between machines; the logistic results reproduce exactly.
