Verification note for the report writer: every number below was extracted from the linked page or PDF during this session through an automated page reader, not by reading tables by hand. Where the reader flagged an internal inconsistency or a truncated document, that is stated next to the finding. Numbers should be re-checked against the paper before being quoted in a final deliverable. Items I could not open or verify are listed under Gaps, never under Cited Findings.

## 1. Einstein, Glick and Palmer: who comments, share opposed, reasons given; did Zoom meetings change participation?

### Takeaway
In 97 Boston-area towns (2015-2017), commenters at housing hearings were older, longer-tenured, more male, far more frequent voters and more often homeowners than the electorate, and 63% of comments opposed new housing against 15% in support. The 2020 online-meeting follow-up found near-identical disparities and positions, so moving online did not broaden participation.

### Cited Findings
- Sample: 97 cities and towns in metropolitan Boston, 2015-2017; 3,123 meeting participants, 2,580 (82.6%) matched to the voter file; logit models on 1,538,086 voters — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick, Perspectives on Politics 2019, author PDF](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf)
- Positions: 63% of comments opposed, 14.6% supported, 22.8% neutral. Support by party: Democrats 19.4%, Republicans 12.8% — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick 2019](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf)
- Commenters vs all voters: age 58.7 vs 50.9; years registered at address 17.4 vs 11.8; female 43.3% vs 51.3%; share of elections voted 50.2% vs 27.2%; no meaningful partisan gap — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick 2019](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf)
- Tenure (Arlington case only): renters are 39% of the town but 22% of participants — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick 2019](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf)
- Reasons in opposing comments: traffic 23.1%, environment 18.6%, flooding 14.9%, safety 14.8%, density 11.9%, aesthetics 11.9%, septic/water 10.9%, neighbourhood character 10.5%, parking 9.9%; affordability about 3% of negative comments. Reasons in supportive comments: aesthetics 11.1%, density 9.7%, affordability 9.5%, environment 9.3% — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick 2019](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf)
- Repeat participation: 83% of commenters spoke at only one meeting; mean 1.3 comments per person; 45 people made five or more comments; repeat participation more common among opponents — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick 2019](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf)
- Benchmark against a ballot measure: across sample towns 56% voted to keep Chapter 40B (affordable-housing law) in 2010, versus about 15% of meeting comments supporting new housing, a roughly 40-point gap; Cambridge 80% vs 40% — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick 2019](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf)
- Outcome evidence in this paper is case-study only (delay and downsizing): a Cambridge 4-unit proposal cut to 3 units after about three months of delay; a Worcester 36-unit proposal cut to 24 and then withdrawn after continuances. No statistical estimate of comment effects on votes — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick 2019](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf)
- "Still Muted" (Einstein, Glick, Godinez Puig, Palmer; working-paper version dated 2 Nov 2021): 798 commenters making 1,078 comments at online housing meetings, March-September 2020; minutes available in 87 of 97 towns, participants matched in 76 — [Still Muted, author PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/607736abaed7201d8e3f9987/t/62f8ff727c2a44568f163961/1660485490895/online_meetings_participation.pdf)
- Online gaps vs voters: +13 points white, +22 points over age 50, +25 points homeowner; authors call these "remarkably similar" to in-person gaps — [Still Muted](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/607736abaed7201d8e3f9987/t/62f8ff727c2a44568f163961/1660485490895/online_meetings_participation.pdf)
- Online positions: 13% support, 61% oppose, 26% neutral (in-person benchmark 14% support). Frequent commenters were less likely to support housing. Differences from in-person: Republicans less likely to participate online; women relatively more supportive online — [Still Muted](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/607736abaed7201d8e3f9987/t/62f8ff727c2a44568f163961/1660485490895/online_meetings_participation.pdf)
- California evidence on remote access (Martin & Venugopal 2026, 115 cities): removing live remote comment after COVID changed public speaker counts by about -1.8 (p = 0.33) or -2.4 with topic controls (p = 0.21), neither significant; pooled demographic effects not significant; heterogeneous by city (whiter cities -5.7 speakers, p = 0.09). Authors conclude lower access costs add speakers but not necessarily more representative ones — [Martin & Venugopal, arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)

### Inferences
- Team finding (2) (most commenters appear once, a small set repeats) is already documented in Boston (83% one-timers) and California councils (73.5% one-timers), so it is a replication, not a novelty. The novel angle would be who the repeaters are by organisation type.
- The team can test "Still Muted" in San Francisco directly: compare composition and support share before March 2020, during remote-only hearings, and after the return to in-person or hybrid.
- The reasons taxonomy (traffic, environment, character, parking; affordability rare among opponents) gives a ready-made coding scheme to compare against SF comment text. An SF-specific expectation worth testing is that displacement and affordability arguments are far more common among SF opponents than the roughly 3% found in Boston suburbs.

### Gaps
- The published journal version of "Still Muted" (I believe Journal of Urban Affairs, 2022/2023) was not opened; numbers above are from the 2021 working paper and may differ slightly in print. The working paper also has an unexplained "36 towns" vs "76 towns" discrepancy.
- The book Neighborhood Defenders (Cambridge UP, 2019) was not opened; only the article was. Book-specific claims are not reported here.
- The widely quoted "homeowners are about 25 points over-represented" figure for the in-person sample was not confirmed from the 2019 article text in this session (the article extract gave tenure only for Arlington).

## 2. Sahn, "Public Comment and Public Policy" (AJPS 2025); Sahn and Simko on issue accountability

### Takeaway
Sahn's San Francisco Planning Commission study (1998-2021, about 42,500 comments, 8,392 voted items) finds commenters are older, whiter and more often homeowners than residents, opponents live closer to projects, and comment balance correlates with Commission decisions even after conditioning on staff recommendation (about +1.7% approval likelihood per positive comment and -2.1% per negative comment), with no causal identification claimed. In contrast to Boston, SF comments are not majority-opposed: 44% of comments on residential-only projects are negative.

### Cited Findings
- Published as Sahn, "Public Comment and Public Policy," American Journal of Political Science 69(2), 2025 — [Sahn publications page](https://alexandersahn.com/)
- Data (2023 manuscript version): SF Planning Commission minutes 1998-2021, 957 meetings, 8,392 project items heard and voted on, 42,451 comments. Polarity recorded by the commission secretary for 31,182 (74%); 2,712 (7%) classified by sentiment model, which matched recorded polarity only 69% of the time in validation. 47.7% of commenters uniquely matched to the voter file — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Volume and balance by project type: residential-only projects average 3.8 comments, 44% negative; office 2.7 comments, 16% negative; commercial 5.2, 32% negative; mixed-use 5.4, 35% negative. 73% of items drew at least one commenter. Among residential projects, the negative share declines as unit count rises — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Demographics: median commenter age 61 vs 46 for registered voters and 38 for the population; women under-represented by about 10%; commenters more than 10 points more likely to be homeowners than residents; Latino and Asian commenters at about two-thirds of population share, white over-represented, Black residents slightly over-represented; commenters voted in about 7 recent elections vs 4 for registered voters — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Proximity: negative commenters live a mean 2.9 km from the project vs 3.8 km for positive commenters (p < 0.01); positive and neutral commenters' distances roughly uniform — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Groups: neighbourhood associations are 5-10% of commenters in a given year, every other group type under 5%; pro-development (YIMBY) groups are the second most numerous organised group and appear from at least 2014; project sponsors and their teams are the largest single speaker category — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Decisions: modal outcome is approval with conditions even when most comments oppose. The Commission followed staff recommendation 56% of the time per the extract, and when it deviated most deviations were against the project (68% in one passage, 74% in another; the manuscript is internally inconsistent) — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Comment-outcome relationship: with staff recommendation and entitlement controls, each extra positive comment is associated with about +1.7% approval likelihood and each extra negative comment with about -2.1% (difference p = 0.06). A one-SD (0.31) rise in proportion in favour is associated with about +0.15 on the approval outcome. Staff recommendation coefficients about 0.44-0.50. Neutral comments unrelated. Nearby commenters' preferences do not correlate with outcomes — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Differential responsiveness: in a joint model the white-commenter coefficient (0.14) exceeds the combined Asian, Black and Latino coefficients (equality rejected, p = 0.04); neighbourhood groups' preferences correlate positively with outcomes. The author states this does not show overt discrimination by commissioners — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Caveats stated by the author: descriptive, turnout endogenous to project viability, no exogenous variation in commenting; continued items excluded from analysis and no continuance rates reported; group classification is a floor — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- A secondary summary reports the same qualitative findings (unrepresentative commenters; closer residents comment more, especially in opposition; white commenters and neighbourhood-group representatives align more closely with approvals) — [California YIMBY research summary](https://cayimby.org/blog/unrepresentative-sample-how-public-meetings-weaken-fair-housing-outcomes/)
- An extended public dataset exists: SF Planning Commission comments January 1998 to March 2026, 1,252 meetings, 25,560 agenda items, 73,002 comments, 28,421 commenters; spoken comment from minutes since 1998 and written comment from correspondence packets since 2017. Position comes from three sources of unequal reliability (secretary's marks, systematic from about 2005; a classifier trained on those marks; LLM coding of written comment). Written comments are attached to the project's first hearing on or after the packet date, and only 37% were heard on the packet date. Name merging is conservative. The README states no empirical findings — [Sahn SF planning public comment data, GitHub](https://github.com/alexandersahn/sf-planning-public-comment-data)
- Sahn and Simko, "Issue Accountability Among Public Meeting Commenters," is listed as conditionally accepted at AJPS — [Sahn publications page](https://alexandersahn.com/)

### Inferences
- The coverage window of the extended dataset (1998 to March 2026) matches the team's dataset description exactly, so the team is very likely working with this build. If so, the codebook caveats apply to their findings: pre-2005 polarity is mostly model-predicted, written-comment polarity is LLM-coded, and written comments are tied to a first hearing, which can distort any analysis of hearing-level outcomes.
- Team finding (1) (denial rare, about 4%; opposition tied to delay and modification) is consistent with Sahn's "modal outcome is approval with conditions" but goes beyond it: Sahn excluded continuances and reported no denial rate. A continuance and modification analysis is a genuine extension.
- Team finding (2) is consistent with Sahn (neighbourhood associations 5-10% of commenters) but Sahn did not report repeat-speaker concentration figures, so concentration statistics (share of appearances by top 1% of speakers) would be new for SF.
- Team finding (3) (tenant-association presence associated with disapproval) is not reported by Sahn, who distinguishes neighbourhood groups and pro-development groups. This appears to be new, with the standing caveat that turnout is endogenous: tenant groups plausibly mobilise against projects involving demolition or tenant displacement, which are also the projects most likely to be disapproved on the merits.
- Sahn's finding that the negative share falls as unit count rises sits in tension with a naive reading of team finding (5) (density-bonus projects draw more opposition). The two are reconcilable if density-bonus use, conditional on size, signals a project exceeding base zoning; the team should condition on unit count and zoning district.
- SF (44% negative on residential) and the California council corpus (slightly pro-development on average, see section 7) differ sharply from Boston suburbs (63% opposed). Sponsor-team speakers and organised pro-housing groups in SF probably account for part of the difference; the team should report balance with and without sponsor-affiliated speakers.

### Gaps
- The published AJPS version was not opened (paywalled); numbers are from the 2023 manuscript on the author's site. Some figures in the published version may differ. The reader flagged two inconsistencies in the manuscript (68% vs 74% of deviations against the project; whether pro-development groups' preferences correlate with outcomes).
- The "followed staff recommendation 56% of the time" figure looks low relative to common expectation of high staff deference; I could not confirm how "followed" is defined (it may refer to exact match including conditions). Treat as unverified until checked in the paper.
- No abstract or findings for Sahn and Simko could be retrieved; the only confirmation is the title and status on Sahn's site. An EUI seminar listing titled "Testing issue accountability among public meeting commenters" surfaced in search but was not opened. I could not verify the claim that this paper concerns written comment specifically.

## 3. Homeownership, renters, institutions: Yoder; Hall and Yoder; Hankinson; Hankinson and Magazinnik; Marble and Nall; Trounstine; Fischel

### Takeaway
Causal and quasi-causal work shows that becoming a homeowner raises local participation (commenting, voting, donating), with larger effects for higher-value homes and when zoning is on the ballot; renters support citywide supply but in high-rent cities oppose nearby market-rate projects at homeowner-like rates; and switching California cities to district elections cut multifamily permitting by roughly half while evening out where housing goes.

### Cited Findings
- Yoder, "Does Property Ownership Lead to Participation in Local Politics? Evidence from Property Records and Meeting Minutes," APSR 114(4), 2020, pp. 1213-1229: uses deed records from California and Texas, an original dataset of individual council-meeting comments, contribution records and voter files; finds becoming an owner increases commenting at council meetings, local voting and donating — [Cambridge/APSR abstract page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/E3BAEB8B52992D8FCF37FF3166BB2E77/S0003055420000556a.pdf/does_property_ownership_lead_to_participation_in_local_politics_evidence_from_property_records_and_meeting_minutes.pdf)
- APSA's summary of Yoder: about 3.5 million people in Palo Alto, Dallas and Houston, 2000-2017; becoming a property owner in Palo Alto makes a person "almost twice as likely" to comment at a council meeting; in 2002, 75% of Palo Alto commenters were property owners — [Political Science Now summary](https://politicalsciencenow.com/does-becoming-a-property-owner-motivate-individuals-to-participate-differently-in-local-politics-in-these-american-cities-the-answer-is-yes)
- Hall and Yoder (working paper dated March 2019; over 18 million voters in Ohio and North Carolina linked to deeds): buying a home raises local general-election turnout by 2.7 to 5.1 percentage points on a base of about 27%; the effect is nearly 4 points larger when a zoning issue is on the ballot; the effect in the top home-price decile is more than twice that in the bottom decile — [Hall & Yoder working paper](https://andrewbenjaminhall.com/homeowner.pdf)
- Hankinson, "When Do Renters Behave Like Homeowners?" (APSR 2018): national survey N = 3,019 in 655 municipalities (July 2016) and San Francisco exit poll N = 1,660 (November 2015) — [Hankinson preprint](https://www.mhankinson.com/documents/renters_preprint.pdf)
- Hankinson, national: 59% of renters vs 28% of homeowners support a 10% citywide supply increase. In a conjoint, homeowners' support for a market-rate building falls about 8 points when it moves from 2 miles to 1/8 mile away; renters overall show no such drop, but renters in top-rent-quintile cities show about a 12-point drop, similar to homeowners (about 10) — [Hankinson preprint](https://www.mhankinson.com/documents/renters_preprint.pdf)
- Hankinson, San Francisco: 84% of renters and 73% of homeowners support a 10% citywide supply increase, yet 62% of renters vs 40% of homeowners support a ban on new housing in their own neighbourhood (gap shrinks to 9 points with demographic controls); among supporters of the citywide increase, 52% of renters and 37% of homeowners still back the neighbourhood ban — [Hankinson preprint](https://www.mhankinson.com/documents/renters_preprint.pdf)
- Hankinson and Magazinnik, "The Supply-Equity Trade-off" (Journal of Politics): 60 California cities that switched to district elections under the California Voting Rights Act, permits 2010-2019; switching reduced logged multifamily units by 0.81 log points (SE 0.46, p about .08), roughly -55%; single-family effect -0.23 (SE 0.24), not distinguishable from zero — [Hankinson & Magazinnik working paper](https://www.mhankinson.com/documents/supply_equity_working.pdf); journal version at [JOP](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/723818)
- Hankinson and Magazinnik, heterogeneity and distribution: declines of about 71-74% in highly segregated cities, cities with a small majority group, and cities with high majority over-representation on council. In a six-city block-group analysis (2011-2018), at-large councils approved about 36% more units in minority than in white block groups; under districts the gap became insignificant (interaction -0.42 log points, p < .001) — [Hankinson & Magazinnik working paper](https://www.mhankinson.com/documents/supply_equity_working.pdf)
- A secondary write-up reports the same 55% average fall in apartment building after district switches — [Sightline Institute](https://www.sightline.org/2022/09/22/when-cities-switch-to-one-winner-council-districts-housing-growth-plummets/)

### Inferences
- Team finding (4) has two halves with different literature support. "Discretionary-review challenges to small projects concentrate in wealthy single-family areas" fits the homevoter logic and the Hall-Yoder gradient (participation effects more than double in the top home-value decile). "Opposition to large projects is higher in lower-priced neighbourhoods" fits Hankinson's price-anxious renter mechanism and the anti-displacement framing, and is not what a pure homevoter model predicts. I found no published study documenting this two-channel split with hearing data, so the combined pattern looks like a contribution.
- Hankinson's SF exit poll (renters more likely than owners to back a neighbourhood ban) offers a direct prior for team finding (3): tenant-aligned opposition to nearby market-rate projects in SF is expected from survey evidence, though it had not been shown in hearing data.
- San Francisco has had district supervisors since 2000, but the Planning Commission is appointed citywide (by the Mayor and the Board President). The Hankinson-Magazinnik mechanism therefore predicts supervisor-level, not commission-level, parochialism; testable by linking projects to supervisorial districts and appeals to the Board.

### Gaps
- Yoder's exact coefficients, number of comments, and comment-topic results were not retrieved (APSR full text paywalled). The "almost twice as likely" figure comes from APSA's summary, not the paper.
- Marble and Nall ("Where Self-Interest Trumps Ideology," JOP 2021) was not searched or opened in this session. From background knowledge, not verified here: liberal homeowners support redistributive housing policy in the abstract but oppose local development at rates similar to conservative homeowners.
- Trounstine (Segregation by Design, 2018; "The Geography of Inequality," APSR 2020) was not opened. From background knowledge, not verified here: restrictive land-use regulation is associated with whiter jurisdictions and persistent segregation.
- Fischel's Homevoter Hypothesis (2001) is a theoretical and qualitative argument that homeowners, as undiversified asset holders, dominate local land-use politics; not opened here, and it does not supply hearing-level effect sizes.
- Been, Madar and McDonnell, "Urban Land-Use Regulation: Are Homevoters Overtaking the Growth Machine?" (Journal of Empirical Legal Studies 2014; New York City rezonings) surfaced in search ([Furman Center PDF](https://www.furmancenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Been_et_al-2014-Journal_of_Empirical_Legal_Studies.pdf)) but was not opened; it is directly relevant to the homeownership-rate gradient in finding (4).

## 4. Delay as the mechanism: CALES entitlement timelines, discretionary review, appeals, attrition

### Takeaway
Berkeley Law's CALES work shows San Francisco's cost is time, not denial: median discretionary entitlement for 5+ unit projects was 27 months in 2014-2017 and 34 months in 2018-2021, versus about 3.4 months for projects using only SB 35 ministerial approval, and discretionary-review requests and continuances are associated with months of added time. The datasets consist of approved projects, so they cannot measure denial or attrition.

### Cited Findings
- Sample: 140 entitlements of 5+ units in 2014-2017 (14,269 units, 15% affordable) and 144 in 2018-2021 (11,339 units, 27% affordable) — [UC Berkeley CALES report for HCD on San Francisco](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- Median time to entitlement for discretionary approvals: 27 months (2014-2017), 34 months (2018-2021); only 5% of 2014-2017 observations were entitled within 12 months; 100% affordable discretionary projects about 15 then about 20 months — [CALES SF report](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- By pathway, 2018-2021 medians: SB 35 only 3.42 months (N=9); discretionary building permit 24.2 (N=17); discretionary building permit with a discretionary-review request 32.33 (N=4); conditional use plus other approvals 39.68 (N=30); development agreement 42.16 (N=4) — [CALES SF report](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- Discretionary review: at least 20% of discretionary building permits in the study years drew a DR request; the with-DR median is about 8 months longer than without (small N) — [CALES SF report](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- Continuances: at least 22% of 2018-2021 quasi-adjudicative entitlements had multiple continuances or hearings for a single step (at least 30% excluding those needing no hearing); one project had nine continuances — [CALES SF report](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- Appeals: at least 14% of entitlements across both periods faced an administrative appeal, usually withdrawn or denied; one withdrawn appeal took 374 staff hours and $52,204 to prepare. The report tabulates 48 CEQA appeals in 2015-2021, 19 of them housing-only — [CALES SF report](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- Structure: among 28 jurisdictions studied San Francisco was the only one making all building permits discretionary through local law; the mandatory preliminary project assessment (required until July 2023) added about a year on average; Berkeley's comparable median was under 10 months with environmental streamlining vs 24 in San Francisco — [CALES SF report](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- The CALES SF dataset contains no denials and reports no denial rate (it is built from approved entitlements) — [CALES SF report](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- Across California city councils 2015-2024, about 1% of recorded votes fail and 87% are unanimous; land use and zoning has the highest share of close votes (3.8%) — [Martin & Venugopal, arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- Statewide CEQA litigation (law-firm-authored, advocacy-oriented study; treat with caution): 498 CEQA appellate decisions 2013-2023, residential projects 26% of challenged projects and the largest category; about 200 CEQA suits filed in superior courts per year — [Hernandez et al., Holland & Knight 2026](https://www.hklaw.com/-/media/files/insights/publications/2026/09/hernandez_2026ceqastudy.pdf)

### Inferences
- Team finding (1) is well aligned with prior work on the "rare denial, long delay" pattern, and the literature leaves the exact piece the team can supply: a denial rate with a proper denominator (CALES conditions on approval; Sahn excludes continuances). A roughly 4% denial rate also sits near the roughly 1% vote-failure rate for California councils overall.
- The unmeasured outcome in all of these studies is attrition: projects withdrawn, abandoned after continuance, or never filed. With item-level histories the team could estimate a competing-risks model (approved, disapproved, withdrawn, indefinitely continued) with opposition volume as a covariate.
- The DR timeline difference (about 8 months) rests on N=4 and N=17; the team's larger DR sample could give the first well-powered estimate of DR-associated delay and modification, and of the geography of DR filers.

### Gaps
- Terner Center reports on approval timelines and discretionary review were not opened in this session; no Terner numbers are reported here.
- The first CALES papers (O'Neill, Gualco-Nelson and Biber, "Getting It Right," 2018, [PDF](https://www.law.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Getting_It_Right.pdf); "Developing Policy from the Ground Up," 2019) were located but not opened; cross-city litigation rates from those papers are not reported here.
- CEQA appeal outcomes and litigation rates in the SF report sit in tables beyond the portion the reader processed (first 100,000 of about 270,000 characters).
- I found no peer-reviewed estimate of project withdrawal or attrition rates in the San Francisco pipeline.
- SF policy changes after 2023 (state-ordered housing element implementation, SB 423 ministerial approval taking effect in SF in 2024, and later local zoning changes) plausibly cut the share of housing reaching the Commission; I did not verify post-2023 timeline data. The team should treat 2024-2026 as a structural break in which projects appear at hearings at all.

## 5. YIMBY mobilisation, labour unions, tenant and anti-displacement groups

### Takeaway
Quantitative hearing-level evidence on organised groups is thin: Sahn documents pro-development groups as the second-largest organised commenter bloc in SF from about 2014, and neighbourhood-level displacement research (Chapple and Song 2024) gives mixed results that both sides of the SF market-rate debate cite. I found no peer-reviewed quantitative study of union testimony or project labour agreements at hearings, nor of tenant-group testimony effects.

### Cited Findings
- In SF Planning Commission minutes, pro-development (YIMBY) groups are the second most numerous organised group after neighbourhood associations and appear from at least 2014; neighbourhood groups' stated preferences correlate positively with outcomes — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Across 96,604 land-use comments at California council meetings, strongly pro-development comments (24,516) outnumber strongly anti-development ones (13,145); supporters mention affordability more, opponents mention traffic, crime, schools, environment and aesthetics more — [Martin & Venugopal, arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- Chapple and Song (JAPA 2024; San Francisco and Los Angeles block groups, 2010-2019, low-income renters): in San Francisco, 100 new market-rate units over three years are associated with about 14% higher short-term odds of low-income out-migration (OR 1.138, decaying to 1.081 longer term) and about 15% higher odds of low-income in-migration (OR 1.153); new subsidised units are associated with about 16% lower short-term out-migration. The reader flagged that some table values differ from text values — [Chapple & Song 2024, Urban Displacement Project PDF](https://www.urbandisplacement.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Can-New-Housing-Supply-Mitigate-Displacement-and-Exclusion-.pdf)
- Renters in San Francisco were more likely than homeowners (62% vs 40%) to support banning new housing in their own neighbourhood in a 2015 exit poll — [Hankinson preprint](https://www.mhankinson.com/documents/renters_preprint.pdf)
- On unions and CEQA, the only source opened offers one qualitative example of a union CEQA suit used to secure a labour agreement and no frequency data; it is an advocacy-oriented law-firm study — [Hernandez et al., Holland & Knight 2026](https://www.hklaw.com/-/media/files/insights/publications/2026/09/hernandez_2026ceqastudy.pdf)

### Inferences
- Team finding (3) is consistent with survey evidence on renter attitudes in SF but appears undocumented at the hearing level, so it is a candidate contribution. The key threat is selection: tenant groups choose which projects to contest.
- Team finding (5) (density-bonus projects draw more opposition): I found no published study testing this. It should be reported as new and conditional on controls for size, height, neighbourhood and affordability share.
- Union participation is an open empirical field. With affiliation codes the team could measure how often building-trades speakers appear, whether they speak in support or opposition, and whether their position switches between hearings on the same project (a signature of a negotiated labour agreement).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed quantitative work on YIMBY group mobilisation at hearings beyond Sahn was found in this session; I did not search sociology or planning journals specifically for YIMBY movement studies.
- No empirical study of project labour agreements, "skilled and trained" requirements or union CEQA leverage with frequencies was verified. Search surfaced only advocacy and trade-association material (for example Associated Builders and Contractors, California Policy Center), which I did not treat as reliable evidence.
- Nothing specific to the 2015 Mission moratorium (Proposition I) debate was retrieved. Related literature located but not opened: Been, Ellen and O'Regan, "Supply Skepticism" (2019) and "Supply Skepticism Revisited" (Housing Policy Debate 2024, [link](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10511482.2024.2418044)); Nall, Elmendorf and Oklobdzija, "Folk Economics and the Persistence of Political Opposition to New Housing" ([PDF](https://priceschool.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Nall-Elmendorf-and-Oklobdzija-1.pdf)); Broockman, Elmendorf and Kalla, "The Symbolic Politics of Housing" ([PDF](https://tobin.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2026-01/BEK_Symbolic_Politics_Housing.pdf)). Their findings are not reported here because they were not read.

## 6. Decision-making by appointed boards: agenda position, meeting length, voting blocs, staff deference

### Takeaway
I found no published empirical study of agenda-order, meeting-length or "decision fatigue" effects on planning commission or zoning board votes; this looks like an open question. The closest evidence is Sahn's finding that staff recommendation is the strongest correlate of SF Commission outcomes and that deviations from staff run mostly against projects.

### Cited Findings
- Staff recommendation coefficients of about 0.44-0.50 in models of SF Planning Commission outcomes; when the Commission departs from staff, most departures (68% or 74%, inconsistent within the manuscript) are against the project — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- California council meetings average about 3 hours with a median of 12 public speakers; 87% of votes are unanimous and about 1% fail — [Martin & Venugopal, arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- The extended SF dataset includes meeting start and end times, commissioner and staff attendance, staff recommendation, Commission action and vote for each item, plus Planning Department staff tenure 2011-2026 — [Sahn SF planning public comment data, GitHub](https://github.com/alexandersahn/sf-planning-public-comment-data)

### Inferences
- Because the data hold item order, meeting times, votes by commissioner and staff tenure, the team could run the first tests of (a) agenda-position and elapsed-time effects on approval, continuance and conditions, (b) commissioner voting blocs by appointing authority (mayoral vs Board appointees), and (c) deference by staff planner experience.
- Agenda order is not random (consent and continuance calendars come first, contested items are often scheduled late), so any order effect needs controls for item type or a design using unexpected delays.

### Gaps
- Two targeted searches for agenda-order or decision-fatigue studies of land-use boards returned only procedural documents. Absence of results is not proof none exists; I did not search law reviews or economics working-paper series exhaustively.
- From background knowledge, not verified here: the general decision-fatigue literature (for example the 2011 PNAS study of Israeli parole decisions) has been challenged on the ground that case ordering is non-random; the same critique would apply to hearings.
- No study of voting blocs on appointed planning commissions was found.

## 7. Recent NLP and LLM work on public meetings (2024-2026)

### Takeaway
Martin and Venugopal (Stanford, arXiv April 2026) is the largest study: about 25,400 California council meetings in 115 cities (2015-2024), transcribed with Whisper and coded with an LLM, finding participants are older, whiter, more male and slightly more often homeowners than voters, that repeat speakers produce most appearances, and that land-use comments lean slightly pro-development on average. It does not estimate effects of comment on decisions.

### Cited Findings
- Martin and Venugopal, "Participation and Representation in Local Government Speech," version dated 22 April 2026: 25,414 meetings, 115 California municipalities including Los Angeles and San Francisco, more than 75,000 hours of audio, more than 150,000 participants, over 100,000 public speakers matched to L2 voter records (about 70% match rate), 96,604 speaker-issue comments on land use and zoning — [arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- Methods: Whisper transcription, pyannote diarisation, Gemini 3 Flash for issue, vote and speaker extraction and ten-topic classification (87% agreement with human labels on government vs public status), name-based race prediction, Callaway-Sant'Anna difference-in-differences for removal of remote comment — [arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- Participants vs registered voters: 4.5 years older (52.6 vs 48.1), 4.9 points less likely female, 5.5 points more Democratic, 16.4 points more likely non-Hispanic white, 3.7 points more likely homeowners (74% vs 70%; homeownership effect shrinks with controls). About 1 in 1,000 registered voters speaks at a council meeting in a year — [arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- Repeat participation: 73.5% of participants appear once, but repeaters account for 62.4% of appearances; the median repeater attends six meetings; repeaters are older, more Democratic and whiter; homeowners are not more likely to be repeaters — [arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- Land use draws the most public engagement: one more percentage point of land-use items on an agenda is associated with about 5.3 more participants — [arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- Stance: mean development stance 0.14 on a -1 to +1 scale (SD 0.61); roughly 0.17-0.19 in 2015-2021 with a modest decline in 2022-2023; traffic shows the largest rhetoric gap between opponents and supporters (about 0.9 more mentions per 1,000 words among opponents) — [arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- City correlates: per-capita participation roughly doubles with a one-SD rise in the share of adults with a bachelor's degree and falls with population and racial diversity; representation gaps persist in high-participation cities — [arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- The paper explicitly leaves the effect of comment on council decisions to future work — [arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)

### Inferences
- Team finding (2) matches the California-wide repeat-speaker pattern closely. A comparable statistic for the SF Commission (share of appearances by repeat speakers, median appearances per repeater) would allow a direct comparison with the 62.4% and six-meeting figures.
- The homeownership gap is much smaller in the council corpus (3.7 points) than in Boston housing hearings (25 points online) or SF planning (more than 10 points). Body type (council vs planning board), matching method and item mix differ, so the team should avoid quoting a single "homeowner over-representation" number.
- Since no large-N study estimates comment effects on decisions outside Sahn's SF correlations, any credible design by the team (for example comparing items before and after exogenous changes in comment access, or same-project repeat hearings) would be novel.

### Gaps
- The arXiv paper is a preprint, not peer reviewed; the reader processed about the first 100,000 of 131,000 characters, so appendix results may be missing.
- LocalView-based studies (Barari and Simko's LocalView database of local meeting videos) were not opened. Two other arXiv papers surfaced but were not read: a data-driven study of public comment across Michigan ([arXiv 2507.18431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.18431)) and "PublicSpeak" ([arXiv 2503.11743](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.11743)). A UC dissertation titled "Political Institutions, Housing Policy, and Large Language ..." ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt21p4n6j2/qt21p4n6j2.pdf)) also surfaced and was not read.

## 8. How the team's five preliminary findings compare, and testable hypotheses

### Takeaway
Findings (1) and (2) replicate documented patterns but add missing measurements (a denial rate with a full denominator; repeat-speaker concentration in SF). Findings (3), (4) and (5) are not documented in the hearing-level literature I could verify, none flatly contradicts prior work, and (5) needs care because Sahn finds opposition share falling with project size.

### Cited Findings
- Already documented: one-time commenters dominate headcount (83% Boston; 73.5% California councils) while repeaters dominate appearances (62.4%) — [Einstein, Palmer & Glick 2019](https://ggwash.org/files/ZoningParticipation_Perspectives_Final.pdf); [Martin & Venugopal 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21202v1)
- Already documented: approval with conditions is the modal SF outcome even under majority opposition; SF entitlement is slow (27 to 34 month medians) and the CALES sample contains no denials — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf); [CALES SF report](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/policy-and-research/plan-report/ucb-examining-local-law-policy-planning-practice-on-development-in-sf-using-cales.pdf)
- Potential tension: among SF residential projects the negative share of comments declines as unit count rises — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Potential tension: nearby commenters' preferences do not correlate with SF outcomes, while white commenters' and neighbourhood groups' preferences do — [Sahn manuscript PDF](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)
- Supporting priors for (3) and (4): SF renters more likely than owners to back a neighbourhood ban (62% vs 40%); homeownership participation effects more than double in the top home-value decile and nearly double when zoning is on the ballot — [Hankinson preprint](https://www.mhankinson.com/documents/renters_preprint.pdf); [Hall & Yoder working paper](https://andrewbenjaminhall.com/homeowner.pdf)

### Inferences
Testable hypotheses for comment-level data:
- H1 (delay vs denial): opposition volume predicts continuance count, time to final action, unit reductions and added conditions more strongly than it predicts disapproval; estimate with a competing-risks or multi-outcome model, and report sensitivity to excluding sponsor-team speakers.
- H2 (attrition): projects with heavy first-hearing opposition are more likely to be withdrawn or continued indefinitely; this outcome is missing from Sahn and CALES.
- H3 (repeat players): repeat speakers are more negative than one-time speakers (as in Boston) and their share of appearances has changed since organised pro-housing groups appeared around 2014.
- H4 (tenant groups): the tenant-association association with disapproval shrinks once project traits are controlled (demolition of existing units, rent-controlled units on site, affordability share), which would indicate selection into contested projects; a remaining effect after controls is the novel result.
- H5 (two geographies): opposition to large projects rises with renter share and rent burden and falls with home values, while DR filings on small projects rise with home values and owner-occupancy; test with tract-level controls and distance from commenter to project where addresses exist.
- H6 (density bonus): density-bonus use raises opposition conditional on unit count, height and zoning district, and the reasons shift toward height, shadow and process. Check whether the effect weakened after state law narrowed local discretion over such projects.
- H7 (remote access): composition and support share did not change materially when hearings went remote in 2020 or when remote comment was curtailed, consistent with "Still Muted" and Martin and Venugopal.
- H8 (responsiveness): replicate Sahn's per-comment associations (about +1.7% and -2.1%) on 2022-2026 data and test whether responsiveness fell as state law (Housing Accountability Act enforcement, SB 330, SB 423) reduced Commission discretion.
- H9 (board behaviour): approval, continuance and condition rates vary with agenda position and elapsed meeting time after controlling for item type; commissioners vote in blocs aligned with appointing authority; deviation from staff recommendation varies with planner tenure.
- H10 (written vs spoken): written commenters differ from speakers in position and geography, and few people use both channels; the dataset README names this as an open question.
- H11 (rhetoric): SF opponents cite displacement and affordability far more than the roughly 3% affordability share in Boston opposition, and rhetoric differs between the two geographies in H5.

Measurement cautions for the team:
- Position coding reliability varies by era and channel (secretary marks from about 2005, classifier before that, LLM for written comment; earlier sentiment classifier agreed with recorded polarity only 69% of the time). Results should be shown separately for secretary-marked positions.
- Opposition is endogenous to project quality and legal vulnerability; all associations with disapproval, including tenant-group presence, are correlational unless a design addresses selection.
- The population of projects reaching the Commission changed after state streamlining laws, so time trends mix behaviour change with composition change.

### Gaps
- I found no published test of density-bonus use and hearing opposition, of tenant-organisation testimony and outcomes, or of the large-project vs small-project geographic split; "not documented" here means not found in roughly 30 searches and fetches, not a systematic review.
- The published AJPS text of Sahn (2025) and the Sahn-Simko paper should be read directly before the team claims novelty on any point, because the manuscript version read here predates publication.
