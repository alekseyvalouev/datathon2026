# Public comment data beats campaign money

The outside data supports Idea 1 (neighborhood defenders) far better than Idea 2 (money in local races), but only if Idea 1 is rescoped from "Bay Area cities" to **San Francisco as the labelled core**. One openly licensed dataset decides the question: Alexander Sahn's parsed San Francisco Planning Commission record, with **73,002 comments on 25,560 agenda items from January 1998 to March 2026**, each carrying a support/oppose/neutral position, a link to the Commission's action and vote, and project coordinates, under CC BY 4.0 ([Sahn repo](https://github.com/alexandersahn/sf-planning-public-comment-data)). That single clone delivers the labels, the outcome and the join key that the Advanced Track asks for, and the coordinates connect directly to the organizer-provided Zillow listings. Idea 2 has one excellent input, San Francisco's nightly public-domain campaign-finance transactions table ([data.gov catalog entry](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/campaign-finance-transactions)), but everything around it is weak: Cal-Access sits at the wrong level of government, San Jose's filings are document-only, the November 3, 2026 results are uncertified until as late as December 3 ([SF Elections](https://www.sf.gov/an-accurate-and-complete-vote-count-takes-time)), and independent-expenditure totals vary by contest rather than by precinct, leaving a few dozen usable observations. Its causal question, separating money that follows winners from money that makes them, cannot be answered in two days with that sample. One caveat governs the whole report: the researchers worked behind a network allowlist and **downloaded no dataset**, so "verified" below means a fact read from a fetched documentation or catalog page, never a confirmed column or row count. Every schema must be checked in the first hour.

## One GitHub repo makes Idea 1 a two-day project

The feared bottleneck for a neighborhood-defenders replication, scraping and hand-coding minutes, is already solved for San Francisco. The Planning Commission's clerk records each speaker with a "+", "-" or "=" mark and a short topic label, and an `ACTION:` line gives the outcome and vote; the researchers confirmed this format on the 24 August 2017 minutes ([SF Planning minutes](https://commissions.sfplanning.org/cpcpackets/20170824_cal.min.pdf)). Sahn's repository parses that record into `comments.csv` (commenter, text, position, channel, linked hearing) and `items.csv.gz` (case number, staff recommendation, Commission action and vote, coded outcome, DataSF project attributes, coordinates), with a codebook and an update script ([Sahn repo](https://github.com/alexandersahn/sf-planning-public-comment-data)). The team's plan of a stance classifier joined to outcomes and neighborhood data maps onto those two files almost one-to-one.

Three properties of the data shape the modelling, and each one is a trap if ignored. First, the `position` label has **three provenances** flagged in `position_source`: the secretary's marks (systematic from about 2005), a classifier trained on those marks, and LLM coding for written comments ([Sahn repo](https://github.com/alexandersahn/sf-planning-public-comment-data)). Training and testing must use only secretary-marked rows; otherwise the team scores its model against another model's output. Second, the spoken-comment text is a clerk's summary, not verbatim speech, so entries such as "Support, maximize housing" leak the label into the features; the researchers flag this as an inference to confirm in `codebook.md`. Third, written comments (2017 onward) contain real prose but carry LLM-assigned labels and attach to the first hearing on or after the packet date, and only 37% of items were heard on that date ([Sahn repo](https://github.com/alexandersahn/sf-planning-public-comment-data)). The honest framing is therefore two tasks: a stance classifier evaluated on human marks, and an item-level model predicting the Commission's action from comment counts, stance balance, staff recommendation and neighborhood covariates. Sahn's own paper supplies a ready benchmark for the first task, since a sentiment-based prediction matched the recorded polarity only **69% of the time** on 27,903 comments ([Sahn paper](https://alexandersahn.com/papers/public_meetings_paper.pdf)).

The cross-city ambition is where the data runs out. Berkeley's Planning Commission minutes record public comment as a bare count ("Public Comments: 42") with no names or positions ([Berkeley minutes](https://berkeleyca.gov/sites/default/files/legislative-body-meeting-minutes/2025-09-17_FINAL%20Minutes_0.pdf)); Oakland posts unstructured PDFs with video on Granicus ([Oakland PC archive](https://www.oaklandca.gov/Government/Boards-Commissions/Planning-Commission/Archive-of-Planning-Commission-Meetings-and-Documents)); and the Legistar API used by San Jose, Hayward, Alameda and San Leandro returns agenda metadata and votes, not coded speaker positions ([Legistar API examples](https://webapi.legistar.com/Home/Examples)). The closest California-wide study, Martin and Venugopal's 115-city council corpus with 96,604 LLM-scored land-use comments, shows no data release ([arXiv 2604.21202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21202)). A second city is achievable only as a small stretch test: captions from five to ten contentious hearings, LLM-segmented and hand-checked, bearing in mind that YouTube blocks caption requests from most cloud IPs ([youtube-transcript-api](https://github.com/jdepoix/youtube-transcript-api)).

Outcome and neighborhood data are the easy half. HCD's Table A records each application's APN, street address, local tracking ID, proposed, approved and disapproved units, a ministerial-versus-discretionary flag and an approved/pending/disapproved/withdrawn status, while Table A2 adds entitlement, permit and certificate-of-occupancy dates ([HCD instructions](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/sites/default/files/docs/planning-and-community/housing-element-annual-progress-report-instructions.pdf)). These tables carry no hearing date or body, so delay must be derived from date gaps. For the core SF analysis they are optional, because `items.csv.gz` already contains the Commission's action; their value is a descriptive cross-city backdrop that keeps some "Bay Area" in the project without any cross-city comment data.

## Idea 2 has one clean table and a broken chain behind it

San Francisco's campaign-finance data is as good as local disclosure gets. The "Campaign Finance - Transactions" table (`pitq-e56w`) combines FPPC Forms 460, 461, 496, 497 and 450, including Form 496 24-hour independent-expenditure reports and Form 460 Schedule D, refreshes nightly, was last updated 11 November 2026, and carries a PDDL public-domain dedication ([data.gov catalog entry](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/campaign-finance-transactions)). Form 496 reports exactly the quantity Idea 2 needs: the candidate or measure, support versus oppose, and the date and amount of each expenditure ([FPPC Manual 1, Ch. 12](https://fppc.ca.gov/content/dam/fppc/NS-Documents/TAD/Campaign%20Manuals/Manual_1/Manual-1-Chapter-12-Reporting-Independent-Expenditures.pdf)). Two limits come with it. The form triggers only at **$1,000 or more within 90 days of the election**, so any timing series is truncated at E-90, and the same spending reappears on later Form 460 filings, a double count the Ethics Commission's own dashboards avoid by using only 496s filed after the latest 460 deadline ([SF Ethics dashboards](https://sfethics.org/disclosures/campaign-finance-disclosure/campaign-finance-dashboards/dashboards-about-the-dashboards)).

The other three sources named in the idea do not hold up. Cal-Access is a state filing system: local committees file with local officers, the Secretary of State offers only a link directory to city and county sites ([SOS directory](https://www.sos.ca.gov/campaign-lobbying/cal-access-resources/city-and-county-electronic-filings)), and San Francisco's dashboards state that state filers are excluded from local data ([SF Ethics dashboards](https://sfethics.org/disclosures/campaign-finance-disclosure/campaign-finance-dashboards/dashboards-about-the-dashboards)). Its replacement, CARS, remains in development with a target of 16 February 2027, so the raw export is at least stable for November 2026 ([SOS CARS updates](https://www.sos.ca.gov/campaign-lobbying/helpful-resources/cal-access-replacement-system-project-cars-updates/cal-access-replacement-system-cars-go-live-updates)). San Jose appears in the state directory under a document-retrieval system, and a 2025 Sightline analysis dropped the city because "its records aren't digitized" ([Sightline](https://www.sightline.org/2025/06/17/in-seattle-elections-dark-money-follows-competition-not-democracy-vouchers/)); the City Clerk now describes a new electronic filing portal, but nothing confirms an export ([San José City Clerk](https://www.sanjoseca.gov/your-government/appointees/city-clerk/elections/campaign-reports)). Oakland is the genuine second city: Public Ethics Commission filings sit on NetFile and sync daily to the city's open data portal ([Oakland PEC](https://www.oaklandca.gov/Government/Boards-Commissions/Public-Ethics-Commission/Disclosure-Information-and-Data/Explore-Campaign-Finance-Disclosures)), and the open-source Open Disclosure pipeline loads Forms 460 and 496 into JSON and CSV ([disclosure-backend-static](https://github.com/caciviclab/disclosure-backend-static)). No statewide machine-readable aggregation of local filings exists; AB 2151 merely obliges agencies to post paper filings online ([FPPC AB 2151](https://www.fppc.ca.gov/learn/campaign-rules/ab2151.html)).

Outcome data breaks the chain in three places. Timing comes first: San Francisco must certify by December 3, 2026 and had issued only preliminary reports through mid-November ([SF Elections](https://www.sf.gov/an-accurate-and-complete-vote-count-takes-time)), while the Statewide Database's newest election is the June 2026 primary, still receiving county updates on 12 November ([SWDB](https://www.statewidedatabase.org)). The freshest spending data therefore has no final precinct results. Coverage comes second: SWDB added local candidate contests only from the 2022 primary and omits local measures ([SWDB FAQ](https://statewidedatabase.org/faq.html)), whereas the academic contest-level database ends in 2021 ([Scientific Data](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41597-023-02792-x)), so the two convenient outcome sources do not overlap. Measures, where San Francisco's largest 2026 committee raised $13.8M ([SF Ethics dashboard](https://campaign.sfethics.org/elections/2026-11-03)), would require registrar files whose precinct identifiers do not match SWDB codes. Sample size comes third. San Francisco ran 13 candidate contests in November 2024 ([SF Ethics dashboard](https://campaign.sfethics.org/elections/2024-11-05/)); a hand-built study of 22 California cities found **46% of races had no independent expenditures at all** ([Common Cause](https://www.commoncause.org/california/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2024/02/CA_AB571_MiniReportv2.pdf)); and Form 496 carries no geography, so precinct results add power only for interaction questions, not for the main effect of spending.

The identification problem is the deeper obstacle. Published work documents that independent spending concentrates in competitive races ([Sightline](https://www.sightline.org/2025/06/17/in-seattle-elections-dark-money-follows-competition-not-democracy-vouchers/)), which is precisely the "follows likely winners" confound the team wants to remove. The credible estimates the researchers located rely on a national policy shock across state legislatures ([Klumpp, Mialon and Williams](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/jle/vol59/iss1/1/)); the one California municipal comparison had 22 cities and mostly insignificant differences ([Common Cause](https://www.commoncause.org/california/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2024/02/CA_AB571_MiniReportv2.pdf)). No peer-reviewed estimate of independent-expenditure vote effects in California municipal races turned up. A weekend Bay Area version yields a descriptive account of where money goes, not an effect.

## Neighborhood covariates are plentiful, so the Zillow join is the real test

Both ideas draw on the same contextual layer, and it is in good shape. The latest ACS 5-year vintage in the Census API is 2020-2024, with detailed tables down to block group ([Census](https://www.census.gov/data/developers/data-sets/acs-5year.html)). The adopted 2026 CTCAC/HCD Opportunity Map offers a statewide summary table and shapefile ([CTCAC](https://www.treasurer.ca.gov/CTCAC/opportunity.asp)), CalEnviroScreen 5.0 was published 30 June 2026 with an ArcGIS feature service ([catalog entry](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/calenviroscreen-5-0-8e575)), and the Othering and Belonging Institute's zoning shapefiles classify land as single-family, multi-family or non-residential across all nine counties as of March 2020 ([O&B Institute](https://belonging.berkeley.edu/about-our-bay-area-land-use-data-sources)). HUD's ZIP crosswalk requires a login ([HUD](https://huduser.gov/apps/public/uspscrosswalk/home)); because the Zillow listings have latitude and longitude, a point-in-polygon join to census tracts sidesteps it. Field names for the Opportunity Map and CalEnviroScreen files remain unchecked.

The Advanced Track's requirement for an organizer-provided dataset separates the two ideas more sharply than any outside source does. In Idea 1, Zillow enters naturally: project coordinates in `items.csv.gz` place each hearing in a neighborhood whose listing prices and rent estimates measure the wealth that the neighborhood-defenders thesis says drives opposition. The weakness is temporal, since listings are current while hearings run back to 1998, so the join is defensible only for recent years or as a stable neighborhood ranking. In Idea 2, Zillow could describe precincts through SWDB's precinct shapefiles ([SWDB geography files](https://statewidedatabase.org/d20/g24_geo_conv.html)), but spending is measured per contest, so the organizer data would decorate the analysis rather than drive it. The remaining rubric items line up the same way.

| Advanced Track requirement | Idea 1 with the data found | Idea 2 with the data found |
|---|---|---|
| Organizer dataset | Zillow price and rent around each project's coordinates | Zillow aggregated to precincts; peripheral to the estimate |
| Trained predictive or NLP model | Stance classifier on secretary-marked comments; item-level approval model | Winner or vote-share regression on tens of contests; no natural NLP task |
| Simple baseline | Keyword or majority-class rule; the 69% sentiment benchmark from Sahn's paper | Incumbency or own-fundraising rule |
| Held-out test set | Thousands of human-marked comments; split by meeting or year | Too few contests for a stable split |
| Three or more subgroups | Spoken versus written, era, project type, opportunity category, Zillow price tercile | Office type, city, cycle; each cell tiny |
| Hand-checked labels or joins | Sample of comment-to-item links, Zillow joins and any LLM-labelled hearing text | Committee-to-candidate matching and 496/460 de-duplication |
| Reproducible code | Clone plus public CSV downloads | Depends on untested NetFile endpoints and registrar spreadsheets |

## Conclusion

The asymmetry between the two ideas is that someone has already done the expensive work for one of them. Idea 1's hard problem, turning decades of minutes into labelled, outcome-linked records, was solved by a published researcher and released under an open licence; Idea 2's hard problem, assembling local filings and precinct outcomes across jurisdictions and then defeating an endogeneity that the literature handles only with national policy shocks, remains unsolved for California cities by anyone the research could find. The recommendation is Idea 1, stated honestly as a San Francisco study: a stance classifier trained and tested on clerk-marked comments, an approval model with Zillow-derived neighborhood wealth, HCD's statewide tables as regional context, and at most a hand-checked handful of hearings from a second city.

The risk worth planning around is not data access but triviality. If clerk summaries give away the stance and nearly every item is approved, both models will post high scores that mean little. The team earns its result by choosing harder targets from the same files, such as stance from written prose with a hand-labelled test sample, or continuances and split votes rather than final approval, and by reporting where the model fails across neighborhoods of differing wealth. If the team still prefers the money question, the only defensible version is descriptive: a San Francisco and Oakland account of when and where independent expenditures arrive, with no claim about votes bought.
